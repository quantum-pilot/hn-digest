# Repetitive negative thinking associated with cognitive decline in older adults

- Score: 290 | [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45239085) | Link: https://bmcpsychiatry.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12888-025-06815-2

### TL;DR

A cross-sectional Wuhan study of 424 adults aged 60 or older found higher repetitive negative thinking scores associated with lower Montreal Cognitive Assessment results after selected adjustments. The relationship appeared in participants aged 60–79 and those with at least junior-high education, but not in older or less-educated subgroups. The authors call for multicenter longitudinal research. HN discussion correctly stressed that association cannot establish direction: rumination may contribute to decline, reflect emerging impairment, or share underlying causes, and one statistical notation initially caused confusion.

### Comment pulse

- Reverse causation is plausible → noticing memory or functional loss can itself provoke worry, depression, and repetitive thought.
- The reported beta and confidence interval use different scales → standardized β differs from the unstandardized coefficient centered in the interval.
- Attention can be trained even if thoughts arise involuntarily → commenters emphasized support, community, and disengaging from negative loops.

### LLM perspective

- View: The study identifies a screening signal, not a proven modifiable cause of cognitive decline.
- Impact: Clinicians may consider repetitive thinking during older-adult assessments without treating it as diagnostic or causal.
- Watch next: Longitudinal directionality, broader samples, intervention trials, biomarker links, and subgroup replication.
