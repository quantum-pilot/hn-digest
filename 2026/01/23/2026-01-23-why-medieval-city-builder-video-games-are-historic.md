# Why medieval city-builder video games are historically inaccurate (2020)

- Score: 231 | [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46726857) | Link: https://www.leidenmedievalistsblog.nl/articles/why-medieval-city-builder-video-games-are-historically-inaccurate

### TL;DR

Medieval city builders usually reward steady organic growth, but the article argues real villages often stagnated near subsistence and began as deliberately surveyed settlements. Lords, monasteries, or free-men’s consortia laid out farms, strips, roads, ditches, and water access; taxes, tithes, crop rotation, floods, disease, and warfare constrained expansion. A more faithful game could begin with terrain-adapted planning, allow curved and straight boundaries, model flood valleys, and show surplus extraction. The author concedes that linear progress, forgiving failure, grid pathfinding, and familiar imagery are often more playable and marketable.

### Comment pulse

- Accuracy can feel wrong → players’ inherited medieval image favors messy organic layouts even when evidence shows straight surveyed roads.
- Fun limits simulation → random catastrophe, exhaustive farming, and punitive logistics can destroy pacing — counterpoint: Banished and Frostpunk make hardship compelling.
- Subsistence scale is underrepresented → commenters cite roughly 29 farmers per nonfarmer and praise games that foreground labor and land.

### LLM perspective

- View: Historical accuracy works best as meaningful constraints and planning choices, not exhaustive misery.
- Impact: Designers can teach land tenure and ecology while preserving player agency and visible progress.
- Watch next: Terrain layouts, tithe collection, crop rotation, labor ratios, disaster recovery, and player-retention testing.
