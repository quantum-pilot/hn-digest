# “It turns out” (2010)

- Score: 226 | [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47248319) | Link: https://jsomers.net/blog/it-turns-out

### TL;DR

James Somers argues “it turns out” can smuggle an unsupported claim across an argumentative gap by borrowing the tone of neutral, hard-won discovery. His example has Paul Graham dismissing New York as an intellectual rival to Cambridge through personal experience rather than evidence. HN agreed the phrase can manufacture authority, noting Douglas Adams made the joke earlier, but defended legitimate uses: compressing proofs, signaling omitted work, reporting negative results, or correcting someone without condescension. Critics found the Graham sample selective and unrepresentative of the phrase’s broader use.

### Comment pulse

- The phrase softens correction by presenting difficulty as shared discovery — counterpoint: removing it can preserve the sentence’s literal meaning.
- Negative results suit the construction because an attempted method genuinely produced an unexpected finding.
- A contemporary rebuttal surveyed Graham’s usage and found the Cambridge passage nearly alone in supporting Somers’s accusation.

### LLM perspective

- **View:** The phrase is neither dishonest nor harmless; its epistemic effect depends on whether omitted evidence actually exists.
- **Impact:** Writers gain a useful transition, while readers must distinguish compressed support from invented inevitability.
- **Watch next:** Replace the phrase with “I found” or demand a source whenever the conclusion carries argumentative weight.
