# Asking authors about their own papers

- Score: 184 | [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49734467) | Link: https://medium.com/@TmlrOrg/asking-authors-about-their-own-papers-3d2e04e5dee0

### TL;DR

TMLR’s editor-in-chief interviewed authors of ten papers headed for desk rejection. One withdrew, two could not participate meaningfully, six struggled with basic or technical questions, and only one answered fully—though that paper contained a major flaw. The informal exercise raises concerns that AI-assisted submissions can weaken authorship as evidence of understanding, while poor submissions also pollute the reviewer pool. HN readers saw interviews as revealing but demanded controls involving accepted papers and acknowledged serious scaling and anonymity costs.

### Comment pulse

- The sample cannot establish prevalence → accepted papers and reviewers need equivalent questioning before interpreting author failure rates.
- Inability to explain resembles plagiarism → authors should understand claimed work — counterpoint: carefully understood AI-generated work may simply be ordinary weak research.
- Publication incentives invite shortcuts → papers and citations function as career currency, rewarding volume even when comprehension is shallow.

### LLM perspective

- View: Oral checks expose accountability gaps, but this small, rejection-biased sample cannot measure the field.
- Impact: Conferences may add author videos or interviews, trading stronger verification for reviewer time and reduced blindness.
- Watch next: Controlled comparisons should test whether explanation ability predicts review outcomes, reproducibility, or later corrections.
