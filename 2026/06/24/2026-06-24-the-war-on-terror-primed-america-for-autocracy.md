# The war on terror primed America for autocracy

- Score: 277 | [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48654367) | Link: https://www.economist.com/by-invitation/2026/06/02/how-the-war-on-terror-primed-america-for-autocracy

### TL;DR

Rosa Brooks argues that 9/11 damaged American democracy less through the attacks themselves than through the response: wars in Afghanistan and Iraq, expanded surveillance and detention, torture, targeted killings without meaningful due process, permanent emergency powers, and growing fear and polarization. These conditions normalized executive overreach before Donald Trump and January 6th. HN commenters largely agreed the Patriot Act’s dangers were foreseeable, while debating whether bin Laden therefore “won,” whether the pattern began decades earlier, and how much emergency action was unavoidable amid credible fears of further attacks.

### Comment pulse

- Post-9/11 powers predictably became permanent → commenters saw repeated Patriot Act renewal, surveillance growth, and torture as proof that emergencies ratchet authority upward.
- Bin Laden won strategically → America depleted legitimacy, cohesion, and finances through overreaction — counterpoint: US regional power expanded, contrary to his stated aims.
- The causal story predates 2001 → commenters traced executive centralization and foreign intervention through the world wars, Red Scares, and earlier domestic violence.

### LLM perspective

- **View:** Terrorism’s democratic damage depends on institutional response; sunset clauses, judicial review, and legislative resistance matter more than threat rhetoric.
- **Impact:** National-security exceptions can migrate into ordinary policing and politics, weakening due-process protections far beyond their original targets.
- **Watch next:** Audit emergency authorities, surveillance retention, targeted-killing safeguards, and congressional renewal rules against measurable necessity and democratic costs.
